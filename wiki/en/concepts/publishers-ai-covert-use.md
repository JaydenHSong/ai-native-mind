# Publishers' Covert AI Use — WIRED Investigation, Staff Revolt (2026-10-09)

## One-line definition

A WIRED investigation reporting that three of the US Big Five publishers have been producing publicity copy, back-cover text, and cover art with ChatGPT and Claude — internally, while publicly posing as generative AI's fiercest opponents.

## Key points

- **Report**: WIRED's Adam Morgan, 2026-10-09. Interviews with 2 dozen+ anonymous staff.
- **Targets**: HarperCollins, Simon & Schuster, and Hachette (three of the Big Five).
- **Uses**: publicity copy, back-cover text, literary-agent emails, marketing materials, cover art, marketing videos — without author consent or disclosure.
- **HarperCollins**: executives bought Anthropic Claude licenses in bulk, then "didn't know what to do with them" (current employee). Junior staff designated as 'AI Champions' with monthly brainstorms. Paid ChatGPT and Jasper use too. One division is "all in" (including AI-generated marketing videos).
- **Simon & Schuster**: OpenAI ran a staff workshop recommending ChatGPT for agent emails. Agents began comparing rejection letters from the same editors and spotting identical machine phrasing (contrast constructions, lists of three).
- **The duplicity**: in public, Google copyright suits and cancelled contracts for AI-suspected authors (Hachette's 'Shy Girl', Macmillan's 'Call Me, I'll Hide the Body' — a $2M advance pulled) — the loudest-opponent posture.
- **Company lines**: Hachette — supports AI for operational purposes, opposes creative/author-facing uses. S&S — limited approved tools, use not mandatory. HarperCollins — no response.

## Why it matters

This is **living evidence of the AI doom loop**: blocking AI training data through lawsuits (defending the upstream supply chain) while stamping out AI-generated copy internally (polluting the downstream supply chain). Two takeaways for the AI-native programmer: first, top-down rollouts like "AI Champions" breed frontline revolt — an organizational-design problem for agent adoption. Second, undisclosed AI copy reaching readers is a real case for the provenance/watermarking debate. And the detail that agents spotted "machine phrasing" in rejection letters shows detection happens socially too — the reader's eye as an alternative to technical watermarking like textGrain.
