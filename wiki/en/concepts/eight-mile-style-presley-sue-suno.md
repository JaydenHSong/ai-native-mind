# Eminem's Publisher and Elvis's Rights Holders Sue Suno on the Same Day — Alleged Unauthorized Use for AI Training, About $41M Claimed in the Eminem Case

## Start here

**Analogy**: the plaintiffs say someone secretly copied 269 of Eminem's songs to build a "song-making machine," then let anyone generate Eminem-style songs with it. The Elvis side goes one step further, arguing the machine was also allowed to imitate the dead singer's voice. None of this has been decided by a court yet — it is what the complaints allege.

| Term | Meaning |
|------|------|
| **Statutory damages cap** | Under US copyright law, up to $150,000 per work for willful infringement — claimable without proving the actual loss |
| **CMI** | Copyright management information — the ownership and source data embedded in a file. Removing it creates separate liability |
| **ELVIS Act** | Tennessee's voice and likeness protection law (2024) — it bars AI from imitating a person's voice or appearance |

## One-line definition

On 2026-11-06, Eight Mile Style — the publisher that administers the rights to 269 Eminem compositions — and a group of Elvis Presley rights holders each sued the AI music generator Suno in the US District Court for the Northern District of California. Same court, same legal team, same day. The Eminem case claims $150,000 per work for 269 songs ($40.35 million) plus $672,000 for removing copyright management information, for a total of about $41 million.

## Key facts

- **The Eminem case (3:26-cv-13399)**: plaintiffs Eight Mile Style and affiliate Martin Affiliated, 32-page complaint. It alleges unauthorized copying of 269 compositions — including "Lose Yourself," "Stan" and "The Real Slim Shady" — for training, plus infringing outputs. Claims include direct copyright infringement, circumvention of technological protection measures, and removal or alteration of copyright management information (CMI). Eminem himself is not a plaintiff.
- **The Elvis case (3:26-cv-13398)**: plaintiffs Elvis Presley Enterprises (an Authentic Brands Group unit), Raleigh Music Publishing and others, 51-page complaint. Beyond the copying of compositions for training and infringing outputs, it claims AI-generated imitation of Presley's voice and identity under Tennessee's ELVIS Act.
- **The central quote**: the complaints cite Suno's earlier court statement that its model was trained on "essentially all music files of reasonable quality that are accessible on the open Internet." The Eminem complaint also alleges that leaked Suno source code shows Eminem music and lyrics scraped from sites such as YouTube and Genius.com (Detroit Free Press).
- **Suno's position**: Suno says it prohibits unauthorized use of an artist's work and that its platform is built for creating new, original music. It denies wrongdoing.
- **The wider fight**: after the RIAA sued Suno in June 2024 on behalf of the major labels, suits from Universal Music Group, Sony Music Entertainment and Round Hill Music are pending. Variety reports Suno raised $400 million in June at a $5.4 billion valuation. The $41 million is what the plaintiff asks for, not what a court has awarded.

## Why it matters (solo-developer view)

1. **Training-data litigation is widening from songs to voices**: the Eminem case is about copyright in compositions, but the Elvis case reaches for publicity rights in voice and identity. The legal risk for music AI is splitting into three lanes — composition, performance and likeness.
2. **A "nearly all music files" statement is now the plaintiffs' centerpiece**: Suno's own earlier court disclosure about the scope of its training data is being recycled as the core evidence against it. It shows how one public statement about training data can return as a liability.
3. **A contrast with the CJEU opinion**: the November 3 opinion from the CJEU's Advocate General treated training as in principle permitted. What US courts answer on the same question is the next thing to watch — both cases are still undecided.

## Limitations (stated)

- Everything here is the plaintiffs' allegation; no court has ruled. The $41 million is a claimed amount, not an award.
- The leaked-source-code and scraping claims come from the complaint and press reporting, and have not been independently verified.
