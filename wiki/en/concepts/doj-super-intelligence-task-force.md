# US Justice Department Launches a Super Intelligence Task Force — Coordinating Criminal and Civil Enforcement Against AI Misuse

## Start here

**Analogy**: as AI-enabled crime grew, the Justice Department bundled what used to be separate criminal and civil teams into one command post. It did not create a new crime — it created an organization to catch AI crime under existing law.

| Term | Meaning |
|------|------|
| **DOJ-SITF** | The Department of Justice's Super Intelligence Task Force, coordinating AI-related investigation and prosecution |
| **Criminal and civil enforcement** | Criminal prosecution (punishing crimes) together with civil litigation (damages, injunctions) |
| **Executive Order 14434** | The September 29 executive order directing federal agencies to say "super intelligence" instead of "artificial intelligence" |

## One-line definition

On 2026-11-05, the US Department of Justice (DOJ) launched the Super Intelligence Task Force (DOJ-SITF), a body that coordinates criminal and civil enforcement against misuse of AI by foreign state actors, transnational criminal organizations and individuals or companies engaged in fraud. It absorbs the AI Litigation Task Force created in January. It creates no new offense or statute.

## Key facts

- **Structure**: co-chaired by Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward and Associate Deputy Attorney General Stephen Weiss, with members from eight department offices including the Criminal, Civil and Antitrust Divisions, the FBI and the National Security Division. It was established by an October 13 memorandum from Attorney General Todd Blanche.
- **Six duties**: (1) organize criminal and civil enforcement involving AI; (2) deconflict investigations among department components and US attorneys' offices; (3) recommend enforcement priorities; (4) serve as the department's main contact with the White House Super Intelligence Force; (5) fold in the January task force and coordinate affirmative civil litigation; (6) develop guidance on prosecuting unlawful AI-related conduct, compliance measures and legislative advice.
- **The name**: Executive Order 14434, signed September 29, directs federal agencies to say "super intelligence" instead of "artificial intelligence" in non-statutory documents. It refers to the existing statutory definition of AI (15 U.S.C. 9401(3)) and creates no new legal category.
- **Limits**: no new offense or statute. Attorney General Blanche has said he will not investigate or prosecute AI companies when no statute is being violated. No case has been announced under the new banner. The next date to watch is November 28, the executive order's deadline for proposed legislative language on a federal AI definition.
- **Relation to the White House task force**: the White House Super Intelligence Force (chaired by Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton) runs through February 12, 2027 and has no enforcement authority of its own. DOJ-SITF is the department's point of contact with it.

## Why it matters (solo-developer view)

1. **"What an agent did" is now an enforcement question**: DOJ-SITF is a declaration that unauthorized agent access, fraud and data theft will be handled as criminal and civil cases, not as technical incidents. The attribution problem in [[concepts/agent-attribution]] now has an investigating organization behind it.
2. **It is old law applied, not new law**: the key point is that no new offense exists. AI companies are not the target — actors who *misuse* AI are. What counts as "adequate controls" is still to be defined in prosecution guidance.
3. **A name change is a policy change**: the term "super intelligence" is spreading across the executive branch. It signals treating AI inside a national-security frame, not just a technology frame. The November 28 legislative-language deadline is the next thing to watch.

## Limitations (stated)

- Only the launch has been announced — no cases, prosecutions or guidance yet. Whether this becomes real enforcement or stays a routing desk is unknown.
- One outlet (nowadais.com) named different co-chairs and an inaugural case, contradicting the DOJ release, Nextgov, PYMNTS and dig.watch; it was excluded as unreliable.
