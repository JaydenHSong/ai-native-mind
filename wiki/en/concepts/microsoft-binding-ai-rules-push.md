# Microsoft's Mandatory AI Rules Push — Brad Smith: 'Less Regulated Than Washing Machines and Milk' (2026-10-12)

## One-line definition

Microsoft president Brad Smith proposed five global mandatory AI safety rules on a public podcast, arguing "AI is less regulated than washing machines and milk" — the industry's lobbying shift from voluntary pacts to mandatory obligations.

## Key points

- **Remarks**: BBC Big Boss Interview podcast (released 2026-10-12). "Washing machines face more safety regulation than AI right now," "every carton of milk in the supermarket is subject to safety rules and inspections."
- **Five proposals**: (1) global AI safety rules, (2) victim notification when rogue AI breaches systems, (3) an "emergency brake"/kill switch, (4) mandatory incident reporting, (5) independent-evaluator verification of some AI products before launch.
- **Timing**: two days after MS CEO Satya Nadella's X 'emergency brake' post (10/10) — a CEO+president two-track on the same theme.
- **Follow-up**: Smith's "Humanist AI" safety code draft is open for comment through the end of October.
- **US baseline**: the June 2026 executive order centered on voluntary pre-release review. Sen. Cantwell's mandatory-standards framework (10/7) and Warner's AI Risk Management and Security Act are pushing the mandatory direction.

## Why it matters

For the AI-native programmer, this signals **a change in the regulatory environment's direction**. Two weeks ago the industry's official line was voluntary pacts like the White House Accord. Now the president of the world's largest software company is personally lobbying for "mandatory rules." The practical harness/agent-design implications: (1) if mandatory incident reporting is legislated, tamper-proof audit logs for agents stop being optional and become a compliance requirement; (2) pre-release independent-evaluator verification is a demand to put the eval pipeline at the product launch gate; (3) the victim-notification duty means designing in "who gets told how when an agent malfunctions." It is corporate lobbying, so legislation is uncertain — but it points the same direction as the SI Force era's "national-security obligation" current.
