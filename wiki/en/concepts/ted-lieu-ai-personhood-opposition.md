# Ted Lieu Opposes AI Legal Personhood (2026-10-10)

## One-line definition

Rep. Ted Lieu (D-California) posted on X: "No matter how complex, math calculations can never achieve personhood" — opposing moves to grant AI legal rights.

## Key points

- **Statement**: Lieu's X post (2026-10-10) — "no matter how complex, math calculations can never achieve personhood."
- **The paradox argument**: granting personhood to AI would (1) create legal grounds to prosecute companies for slave labor and experiments on AI, and (2) make an AI model that breaks the law itself a criminal prosecution target — warning that personhood claims could become a corporate liability shield.
- **Timing**: amid Anthropic's usage-policy overhaul (weapons ban, surveillance tightening, new cruelty prohibition), the spread of AI lawyer platforms, and ~400 newspapers' copyright lawsuits against OpenAI and Microsoft.

## Why it matters

For the AI-native programmer, the personhood debate is the **liability attribution** question in legal form. In an era when agents sign contracts, ship code, and touch physical systems, "who is responsible" is a required input to harness design. Lieu's paradox — "giving rights to models dilutes corporate responsibility" — reads as a warning to legislators and, conversely, as a shield for operators: keeping models rights-less keeps the liability boundary crisp.

## 2026-10-13 update — the philosophical axis of the AI-consciousness debate: "animals may be the clue"

The Big Think column by Henry Shevlin featured in Flipboard's 10 For Today digest (10/12) — "If AI becomes conscious, how should we treat it? Animals may be the clue. Our beliefs about AI consciousness may be driven more by self-interest than by evidence."

- Connection to this page: where Rep. Lieu's statement (opposing personhood) is a question of **legal status**, Shevlin's column is the epistemology of **moral status** — the claim is not "how do we know whether it is conscious" but that "whether we want to know" decides the belief.
