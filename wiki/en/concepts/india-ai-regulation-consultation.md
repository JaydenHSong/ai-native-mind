# India's AI Regulation Consultation Paper — Deepfakes, Agentic AI, and the Techno-Legal Approach (Due Within a Month)

## One-line definition

On 2026-10-08 India's electronics and IT minister Ashwini Vaishnaw announced a consultation paper on AI regulation due within a month — AI safety first, covering deepfakes, autonomous-agent risk, and skilling. Still a draft stage, not finalized regulation.

## Key points

- Announcement: Oct 8 in New Delhi, at the World Bank's World Development Report 2026 launch (this year's theme: AI). Vaishnaw directed his department to draft a "regulation-focused" consultation paper.
- Five scope axes: ① AI safety (safety of AI use is the top priority), ② deepfakes and synthetic media, ③ AI harms (cyber threats, mental-health effects), ④ a human-centered approach, ⑤ skilling and reskilling ("no one left behind").
- On deepfakes: a "nuisance which can cause huge problems in society" — quality improves daily, people believe them and act wrongly on them. IT Rules amendments already cover AI-generated content; the paper takes a wider view of the risks.
- Regulatory philosophy — **techno-legal**: "AI cannot be governed through legislation alone. We have to bring a techno-legal solution" — stakeholders must be on board, and industry bears primary responsibility for tackling risks.
- Strategic autonomy: MeitY Secretary S Krishnan — proprietary foreign models carry the risk of Indian data leaving the country and being learned from → build domestic AI capabilities.
- Infrastructure in parallel: ~5,000 GPUs in initial procurement (estimated need: 10,000), plus a panel-based public-procurement model for AI suppliers.
- Background: follows this year's India AI Governance Guidelines (principle-based: trust, people-first governance, responsible innovation).
- Status: the paper will form the basis of a future regulatory framework — not finalized regulation.

## Why it matters

- **The four-axis regulatory front**: in the same week — EU-style law (textGrain/EU AI Act) + UK ICO-style supervisory agreement (10/8) + U.S. mandatory reporting (10/9) + India's consultation launch (10/8). Global regulation moved in the same week in four different grammars.
- **Techno-legal differentiation**: unlike the EU's law-first approach, India names "technology + law" explicitly — technical measures like watermarking and detection become part of regulation. Demand-side grounding for [[concepts/ai-text-watermarking]].
- **Data sovereignty, stated plainly**: "foreign models learning from Indian data" as a minister-level concern — data offshoring is becoming a procurement criterion in model selection.

## 2026-10-08 update — Australia's 'systems-based' regulation: the third regulatory grammar

On the same day (10/8), Assistant Minister Andrew Charlton announced Australia's 'systems-based' frontier AI approach — evaluating whether "rigorous processes" for finding, testing, reporting, and managing risk actually work. Voluntary codes are set aside for the most severe frontier risks; detailed prescriptive rules are rejected as too slow for the technology's pace. National AI standard this year, legislation in 2027, with the ISO/IEC 42001 evidence structure.

→ Three regulatory grammars are now complete: (1) EU-style law and prescriptive rules, (2) India's techno-legal mix, (3) Australia's **evaluation of process operation**. The "four-axis regulatory front" is now five axes (EU, UK, U.S., India, Australia). The moment you stand on the frontier-model-building side, "model evaluation records, risk-test logs, reporting processes" — **evidence-generation** infrastructure — becomes the core of regulatory response. Details: [[concepts/australia-frontier-ai-regulation]].
